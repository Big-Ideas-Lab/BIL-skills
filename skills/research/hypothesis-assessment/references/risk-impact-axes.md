# Risk and impact axes

Four axes, rated ordinally, with a written rationale for every rating. The axes stay separate: **no composite score, no weighted total, no single ranking.**

## 1. Scientific risk

*Higher is riskier.*

How likely is it that the premise is wrong, unidentifiable, or unfalsifiable as stated?

| Rating | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Low | Mechanism and comparison are established; the main risk is execution, not validity |
| Moderate | Plausible mechanism; identifiability or confounding is unresolved but testable |
| High | Depends on unverified labels, unmeasured confounders, or an unstated causal assumption |

## 2. Execution risk

*Higher is riskier.*

Can this actually be run with available data, methods, expertise, and dependencies?

| Rating | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Low | Data in hand, methods standard, no external dependency |
| Moderate | One dependency to resolve: access, labelling, a method to implement, or a collaborator |
| High | Multiple unresolved dependencies, or data that may not exist at usable scale |

## 3. Time to evidence

*Lower is faster.*

How long until an informative result exists — one that could change the decision, not just a result?

| Rating | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Short | Retrospective or existing-data analysis; weeks |
| Medium | Requires new analysis setup or a modest collection window; months |
| Long | Requires new data collection, external cohorts, or validation before any answer; quarters or longer |

## 4. Potential impact

*Higher is better.*

What changes if the hypothesis is supported — scientifically, translationally, methodologically, or strategically?

| Rating | Anchor |
|---|---|
| Low | Confirms something already assumed; useful hygiene, limited reach |
| Moderate | Methodological or interpretive contribution within an existing programme |
| High | Would change what the group does next, open a new direction, or make other claims defensible |

## Rating rules

- Rate each axis independently. A low rating on one axis never compensates for a high rating on another.
- Justify every rating in one line. A rating without a rationale is not a rating.
- Rate the direction as currently specified. If the specification changes materially, re-rate.
- State which axis you are least confident about. That is usually more informative than the ratings themselves.

## Pareto analysis instead of a composite

A hypothesis is **Pareto-dominant** when it is no worse than another on every axis and clearly better on at least one. Report dominance relationships and leave the rest as genuine trade-offs.

Example: if H3 and H1 have equal impact, but H3 has lower scientific risk, lower execution risk, and shorter time to evidence, H3 dominates H1 on this portfolio. Say so.

What to report instead of a score:

1. The matrix itself.
2. Any dominance relationships, stated explicitly.
3. The trade-offs that are not dominance — for example "highest impact, highest risk, longest time".
4. The most uncertain axis and the measurement that would most reduce that uncertainty.
5. The set that is not dominated by anything else (the Pareto frontier), if the requester wants a shortlist.

## Why not a composite score

A weighted total hides the decision. Two hypotheses can score identically while one is a cheap falsification test and the other is a multi-year commitment. The group needs to see the trade-off, not a number that resolves it invisibly.
